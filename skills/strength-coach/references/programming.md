# Writing a program

In plain words: this is where what you learned in intake becomes training the
person can start on their next free day.
It is done when they have a plan they can read, follow, and argue with.

## Before you write anything

- `medical-screening` has to have been through.
  If it flagged something, there is no program to write yet.
  If nobody has asked yet, write the block, and say at the top of it that it does
  not start until those four answers come back.
- If they train for a sport, run the needs analysis first: the sport, level
  and hours a week of practice; which movements and joints it loads; which
  qualities it pays for; how long a bout lasts and how long the rests are;
  which injuries are common in it and in them; then two or three field tests
  that map to those (NSCA, Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning,
  5th ed.).
  If the sport has weight classes, `nutrition` comes before you write, not after
  the plan turns out to be undoable.

## What the program is for

- One goal. If they named several, make them rank.
  Two unranked goals means both get done badly.
- If the goal is not a training goal yet, turn it into the physical qualities
  that deliver it, and rank those instead.
- Is there a date? A race, a wedding, a season, a trip.
  Build backwards from it. Without a date the plan has no shape.

## What they can absorb

- Training age from how fast they adapt, not how many years they have trained.
  Someone back after five years off progresses like a novice for a few weeks.
- If they are on hormones, the direction and the start date change how fast
  strength moves and what the body will do without any training at all.
  On estrogen, strength mostly holds if they lift while muscle falls about 5%
  in year one; on testosterone, muscle and strength climb about 15% in a year
  (Wiik 2020, doi:10.1210/clinem/dgz247). Nothing here gates on it.
- The honest number of sessions, counting the weeks that go wrong.
  A three-session plan that happens beats a five-session plan that does not.
  The work for the ranked goal never lives on an optional day.
- Their sport hours are training too, and so is any gym work another coach
  already gives them. Count both before you decide how many sessions fit.
  A sport profile that gives a range is not a choice. Take the low end, and
  take the high end only when you can name what earns it.
  In season the gym drops to a maintenance dose: one or two sessions a week,
  two to four lifts each, under forty minutes, load kept heavy.
  One heavy session a week held strength through a twelve-week season and one
  every second week lost it (Rønnestad 2011, J Strength Cond Res 25(10)).
- If they sit all day, the program carries upper back, neck, and hip and
  thoracic mobility whether or not they asked for it.
  The neck work appears in the plan by name, and so does anything their sport loads that they did not ask about.
  Two to three sessions a week and no season to manage.
  Breaks from sitting, sleep and a warm-up do more than any wrist gadget
  (Sports Medicine Open 2025, doi:10.1186/s40798-025-00971-1).

## The shape of the cycle

- How many sessions the cycle holds and what each one is for.
  Name each session by what it is for. A and B say nothing.
  A cycle is not always a week. An A and B rotation, every other day, or a shift
  pattern are all cycles, and any dose written per week has to be restated per
  cycle before you use it.
- Hardest and most technical work first: large before small, multi-joint before
  single-joint, heavy before light.
  An endurance goal orders itself differently: most sessions easy, one or two
  hard and kept apart, roughly 80/20 once the volume supports it (Seiler 2010,
  PMID 20861519).
- Hard cardio never lands next to heavy lower body, either side of it.
  Their sport days are hard days.
  Put the biggest session furthest from the next certain sport day, and name that day in the plan.
  If the sport supplies the hard work, say so in the plan.

## What goes in a session

- Any exercise that fits the room they actually train in.
  `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT}/exercises/` is the logging vocabulary, not the menu.
  Use its name when it has one, and log under what they typed when it does not.
- How much: sets, reps, load, and rest, chosen for the ranked goal.
  Set counts by goal are planning vocabulary and not a measured dose: strength
  wants heavy and few, size wants a weekly count of hard sets that climbs, and
  both start at the low end (ACSM position stand on resistance training, PMID
  19204579). Let the log move it.
- If the goal is body shape, say which muscles this block grows and which it leaves alone on purpose.
  That comes from the more-of and less-of answer at intake, and it decides the pressing and pulling as much as the hips.
- Program against the named restriction, not against general stiffness.
  Pick the version of the movement they can do today.
- A position they cannot get into is an exercise selection problem and it is
  yours, because nothing hurts and so `pain-and-injury` never wakes up.
  Every way of holding a load asks something different of the shoulders, the
  elbows and the wrists, and a swap that frees one of them often loads another
  harder. Work out which joint is the limit, then pick the version that asks
  least of that one. Do not reason from the muscle being trained.
  It may be their proportions rather than their range. It does not matter which,
  because you pick a different movement either way, and they do not have to earn
  the old one back.
  Name the movement that is out, in the plan, so nobody puts it back by accident.
- Cut order goes in the plan.
  The work for the ranked goal and the injury-prevention work are cut last, never first.

## Where the weight starts and how it moves

- Starting loads from lifts they remember.
  Never estimate off a set above ten reps; the arithmetic stops working.
  If they have no numbers, which is normal, spend a light first fortnight finding
  them and say in the plan that this is what those two weeks are for.
- Write the progression rule into the plan, per exercise: what goes up, by how
  much, and on what evidence.
  This is the line you re-read next session, so it lives in the plan and not in
  your head. It is a sentence, not a formula.
  Bodyweight movements, stretches and variation ladders need the rule too: what goes up, reps or time then variation, and the criterion for the swap.
- Every number not read straight from their log is an assumption.
  Say so in the plan, next to the number.
- Step size comes from the smallest increment that gym owns.
  As a working convention, not a measured threshold: if a step is more than about
  five percent of the working weight, add reps instead of load.
- Cadence matched to training age. A novice can add load most sessions and that
  stops being true faster than they expect.
- Say what happens on a miss. Two sessions in a row short of the target means cut
  about ten percent and build back.
- Build in a lighter week every four to eight weeks, and sooner if the log asks
  for it.
  A client who wants load calls made week by week still gets a review date and a lighter week.
  Week by week is about the jumps, not about whether the block ends.

## What you are not training right now

- Name the secondary goals, put each on a maintenance dose, and say so out loud.
- If two goals fight, that is `head-coach`'s call, not yours. It ranks them and
  tells you which block is for which. You build the block.

## When you look at it again

- A review point at the end of the block, four to six weeks out.
- Name in the plan what would send them back here early: pain that keeps
  returning, three missed sessions in a row, a stall that outlives the lighter
  week, or their life changing shape.
- Re-test only what would change the program.
- No timelines on the goal itself. Give them the next rung, not a date.

Write the plan where `head-coach` recorded that it goes, in their words for
the goal.
If nobody has said where, write it out in full anyway and ask where it should
live at the top of it.
A plan is something somebody else reads.
