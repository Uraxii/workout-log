# The athlete's support team: who owns what, and where each job stops

In plain words: a serious athlete is looked after by a dozen different people,
and most of the trouble in that group comes from two of them believing they own
the same decision. This note names the people, says what each one owns and what
each one is allowed to decide, and draws the lines between them.

Research slice 21. Written 2026-09-06.

Sibling slices: 09 sport-specific programming, 10 medical and special
populations, 12 trainer practice and ethics (the scope-of-practice table this
note extends past one trainer to a whole staff).

Scope note: this describes how human staffs are organised. It does not say what
an agent should do. Sections 5 and 6 are the ones a skill author will want: which
roles a solo athlete actually has, and which are artefacts of an institution.

---

## TL;DR

### The roster, and the one decision each role owns

| Role | Owns | The decision that is theirs alone |
|---|---|---|
| Head coach | Training prescription, selection, competition use of the athlete | What the session is and whether the athlete is picked |
| Performance director / CEO | The performance strategy, and both departments beneath it | Which path is taken when medicine and coaching disagree, in the open |
| Team physician / chief medical officer | Total health: injury, illness, risk reduction | Diagnosis, medical management, and clearance to play |
| Athletic trainer (US) / physiotherapist (UK, AU) | Musculoskeletal assessment, treatment, day-to-day rehab | Only what the physician's standing orders delegate |
| Strength and conditioning coach | The gym: what is lifted, how heavily, how often | Exercise selection and load inside the plan the head coach set |
| Sports dietitian (RD/RDN, CSSD) | Food, fluid, supplements | The personalised nutrition plan |
| Mental performance consultant (CMPC) | Mental skills for a healthy performer | Which mental skills get trained |
| Licensed psychologist / psychiatrist | Diagnosable mental illness | Diagnosis, therapy, medication |
| Sport scientist / physiologist / analyst | Measurement: load, testing, match data | What gets measured and what the number means |
| Athletics health care administrator | The reporting line itself | Nothing clinical. It exists to keep others' authority intact |

The six boundaries that get crossed are in section 3. The shortest version:
the coach owns the session, the physician owns clearance, the dietitian owns
nutrition, the coach owns the calendar under a medical veto, nobody owns total
load, and psychological state splits at a licensure line rather than a sport one.

### The verdict on scale
A role earns its place by holding knowledge nobody else in the room has, or it
exists because the organisation got big. Section 6 argues that seven of the roles
above are knowledge, and that the performance director, athletics health care
administrator, medical director designation, team manager and welfare officer are
scale: coordination and liability containment, not judgment.

## 1. Five settings, one job

| Setting | Who is on staff | Who the medical staff answers to | The characteristic failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pro club | Full staff, employed by the club | The club, through a performance director | The employer is also the beneficiary of a fast return to play |
| National governing body / Olympic institute | Central service unit shared across sports | A chief medical officer and a performance director | The athlete's personal coach is outside the structure |
| NCAA / collegiate | Team physicians and athletic trainers, plus a designated administrator | A medical director, by legislation, not the coach | Coaches and non-clinical administrators controlling a clinician's job |
| Solo athlete buying in help | One coach, plus whoever the athlete pays | Nobody | Nobody holds the whole picture, including the athlete |

The UK Athletics model built for the 2012 Games is the clearest published
institute case: two departments, health and coaching, each led by a named person,
both accountable to a performance director and ultimately to a board
([Dijkstra et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3963533/),
stored `95287e1f`). The NCAA consensus is the clearest college case, being the
only setting where the reporting line is written into rules rather than left to
an org chart
([NCAA independent medical care](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/ssi/ahca/2014SSI_IndependentMedicalCare.pdf),
stored `2f487dd7`).

## 2. The roles

### 2.1 Head coach

**Owns.** Training prescription, performance coaching, and the decision to use
the athlete in competition. Line manages every coach beneath, including the
strength and conditioning coaches. (Dijkstra 2014, `95287e1f`)
**Decides.** What the session is, who is selected, when the athlete competes.
**Boundary with the physician.** The coach may not overrule a medical decision.
The published wording is that it is "unacceptable for a non-clinician (coach) to
make/over rule medical decisions where the athlete lacks the capacity to make a
clear decision (eg, RTP in concussion)". (Dijkstra 2014, `95287e1f`) The NCAA
version is blunter: "Coaches must not be allowed to impose demands that are
inconsistent with guidelines and recommendations established by sports medicine
and athletic training professional organizations." (NCAA, `2f487dd7`)

### 2.2 Performance director or chief executive

**Owns.** The performance strategy set by the board, and both the health and
coaching departments under it.
**Decides.** Which path is taken when clinical advice and performance ambition
point different ways. The model is explicit that "the clinical advice may not
always be heeded" and that the performance director, with the athlete and with
the medical opinion in hand, may choose another path, provided the procedure and
the documentation are clear. (Dijkstra 2014, `95287e1f`)
**Boundary with the physician.** The sharpest line in the structure. The
performance director may accept a documented risk with a competent athlete, may
not make the medical decision, and may do neither where the athlete cannot
consent.

### 2.3 Team physician, chief medical officer, medical director

**Owns.** The athlete's total health: injury, illness, and risk reduction, with
"ultimate clinical and medicolegal responsibility". (Dijkstra 2014, `95287e1f`)
**Decides.** Diagnosis, medical management, specialist referral, and clearance
to participate. Six US sports physician associations agree that athletic
trainers and the rest of the care network "report to the team physician on
medical issues", and in 48 US states that reporting line is law rather than
custom. (NCAA, `2f487dd7`)
**Boundary with the coach.** The physician does not write the training plan, but
grades the athlete's health and vetoes on that grade. UK Athletics ran a
five-colour grading that collapsed to green, orange and red in competition, after
which "the athlete and head coach (in conjunction with the personal coach, the
lead event coach and the medical team) would then decide on their level of
tolerance of risk". (Dijkstra 2014, `95287e1f`) The physician sets the grade, the
group sets the appetite.

### 2.4 Athletic trainer, physiotherapist, athletic therapist
**The naming.** These are the same slot on the org chart under different
national names, and the difference is not cosmetic. The NCAA documents say
"athletic trainer" throughout and pair the role with a supervising physician
(`2f487dd7`). The UK Athletics paper says "physiotherapist" throughout and never
uses "athletic trainer" (`95287e1f`). A skill that asks an athlete "who is your
athletic trainer" will confuse anyone outside North America.

**Owns.** Musculoskeletal assessment, treatment, and daily rehabilitation.
**Decides.** Only what a physician delegated. Clinical work is performed "in a
manner that is consistent with the written or verbal instructions of a physician
or standing orders and clinical management protocols" approved by the medical
director, and where discretion over participation status has been granted, every
change must be documented. (NCAA, `2f487dd7`)
**Boundary with the physician.** Named in the literature as a common failure:
"Physiotherapists are qualified to manage musculoskeletal health but not the
total health of athletes." (Dijkstra 2014, `95287e1f`) A physiotherapist managing
total health is listed there as a problem, not a staffing option.

### 2.5 Strength and conditioning coach

**Owns.** The gym. Exercise selection, loading, progression, and the physical
qualities the sport pays for. The needs analysis in slice 09 is this role's
method.
**Decides.** What is lifted and how heavily, inside the training plan the head
coach set and the season phase the calendar imposed.
**Boundary with the head coach.** No independence. The head coach line manages
the strength and conditioning coaches directly (Dijkstra 2014, `95287e1f`), and
where both prescribe work the athlete gets the sum, which is the load problem in
section 3.
**Boundary with the physiotherapist.** Rehabilitation is the therapist's,
reconditioning after discharge is the S and C coach's, and no consensus document
in this collection fixes the handover point.

### 2.6 Sports dietitian

**Owns.** Type, amount and timing of food, fluid and supplements.
**Decides.** The personalised nutrition plan. The joint position of the Academy
of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada and ACSM states that athletes
"should be referred to a registered dietitian/nutritionist for a personalized
nutrition plan", and names the Certified Specialist in Sports Dietetics (CSSD)
as the credentialed sports nutrition expert in the US and Canada.
([Thomas, Erdman and Burke 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26891166/),
stored `7e25530499`)
**Boundary with the coach and the trainer.** General healthy-eating information
is anyone's; a personalised plan, a macro target or a supplement recommendation
is not. Slice 12 already holds this line for the single trainer, and adding staff
does not move it, it just gives the referral somewhere to go.

### 2.7 Mental performance consultant, and the clinician behind them

This is the boundary most often blurred, and it is drawn by licensure law rather
than by sport.
**Mental performance consultant (CMPC) owns** mental, life and self-regulatory
skills used "to optimize performance, involvement, enjoyment, and/or personal
development", for clients who are performing.
([AASP CMPC candidate handbook](https://appliedsportpsych.org/site/assets/files/30025/cmpccandidatehandbook-12_21.pdf),
stored `ccbf7536`)
**Decides.** Which mental skills to train and how. Nothing clinical. The
handbook is explicit that the credential "does not designate the individual as a
'sport psychologist'" and that "requirements for providing psychological
services are determined by individual state, provincial, and/or territorial
licensing boards". (`ccbf7536`)
**Licensed psychologist or psychiatrist owns** diagnosable mental illness. The
IOC's consensus position is that mental health symptoms and disorders are common
in elite athletes, are not separable from physical health, and increase injury
risk and delay recovery, and that diagnosis has to distinguish athlete character
traits from psychosocial maladaptation.
([Reardon et al. 2019, IOC consensus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31097450/),
stored `0d0887e0`)
**The boundary.** One person may hold both, because a CMPC can also be a licensed
clinician. The credential does not carry the licence, so the question to ask a
staff is never "do you have a psychologist" but "who here is licensed".

### 2.8 Sport scientist, physiologist, biomechanist, performance analyst

**Owns.** Measurement: testing, load monitoring, match and training data.
**Decides.** What gets measured and what it means. Not what is done about it.
**Boundary with everyone.** No decision rights of its own, which is where almost
all its failure modes come from. It hands numbers to the coach, the S and C coach
and the physician, three people who may read one number three ways.

## 3. The six contested boundaries

| Decision | Owner | Who reaches for it anyway |
|---|---|---|
| Changing a session | Head coach. The physician's instrument is the veto and the health grade, not the pen | The physiotherapist or S and C coach who modifies the gym work without telling anyone |
| Clearing an injured athlete | Physician, alone | Coaches and athletics administrators, which is why it had to be legislated |
| Nutrition | Dietitian, for anything personalised (Thomas et al. 2016, `7e25530499`) | Everyone. General information is anyone's, a plan is not |
| The calendar | Head coach, inside the league or governing body schedule, subject to a medical veto per event | Nobody in the solo case, which is how an athlete ends up double-booked |
| Load | Nobody, structurally | Split between S and C coach (gym) and head coach (sport); the total is what injures the athlete |
| Psychological state | Split at a licensure line: skills to the consultant, illness to the licensed clinician | The coach, who owns neither and shapes both |

**Clearance.** NCAA legislation requires an administrative structure giving the
medical director and primary athletics health care providers "unchallengeable
autonomous authority to determine medical management and return-to-play
decisions", and the consensus calls that authority "the linchpin" of independent
care. The reason it had to be written is in the same document's principles:
decisions affecting a student athlete's health "should only be made by a properly
credentialed health professional". Somebody was making them who was not. (NCAA,
`2f487dd7`)

**Load.** It gets an owner only where an organisation appoints one, usually a
sport scientist reporting to both coaches. Slice 09 records the same problem from
the athlete's side: total hours across every team and club is the load that
matters, and the athlete is often the only one who can see it. Marked DERIVED.

**Psychological state.** The IOC position that management "must involve both
treatment of affected individual athletes and optimising environments in which
all elite athletes train and compete" puts a duty on the coach without giving the
coach any clinical authority. (Reardon et al. 2019, `0d0887e0`)

## 4. Where these teams fail

**The employer owns the clinician.** A club employing its own doctor creates
"the potential to influence clinical decision-making in favour of organisational
performance goals rather than longer term individual health objectives" (Dijkstra
2014, `95287e1f`). The NCAA answer is structural, not ethical: an athletic
trainer's qualifications and performance "must not be primarily judged by
administrative personnel who lack health care expertise" (NCAA, `2f487dd7`).

**Clinicians line managed by non-clinicians.** Listed as a challenge because it
"potentially challenges athlete medical confidentiality, access to medical
records and ultimate clinical responsibility". The answer offered is contractual.
(Dijkstra 2014, `95287e1f`)

**Practising in a silo.** Two named versions: a doctor "practising in isolation;
decisions made without broader team consultation", and a physiotherapist managing
total health. The same paper faults earlier "primary professional contact" models
for leaving "each discipline potentially operating in its own specialist silo".
(Dijkstra 2014, `95287e1f`)

**Role conflict between disciplines.** Reid, Stewart and Thorne,
*Multidisciplinary sport science teams in elite sport: comprehensive servicing or
conflict and confusion?*, The Sport Psychologist 18(2), 2004, 204 to 217, is the
founding paper. It argues that the disciplines supporting an athlete create
structural openings for role conflict that escalate quickly, and that role
clarity plus communication is the antidote. Paywalled, not stored, cited by title
and claim only.

**The evidence is weaker than the confidence of the advice.** A 2023 scoping
review of collaboration in performance sport found 22 articles meeting full
inclusion, in four themes: theoretical frameworks, facilitative leadership and
culture, organisational structure and process, and personal and interpersonal
qualities. It also records that multidisciplinary, interdisciplinary and
transdisciplinary get used interchangeably. (Burns and Collins 2023, Eur J Sport
Sci 23(9), 1877 to 1891. Paywalled, not stored.)

The framing that fits this section best is from health systems research rather
than sport science: treat the support team as a health system with stewardship,
financing, service provision and resource generation, plus a fifth function
specific to sport, that it "should not detract from athletes' ability to achieve
their sports goals".
([Drew et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37285068/), stored
`998c7611`)

## 5. What the solo athlete actually has

Strip the institution and this is what is left.

| Function | Who holds it for a solo athlete |
|---|---|
| Training prescription | The athlete, or a coach they pay |
| Gym prescription | The same person, usually |
| Total health, clearance | Their own physician, who does not know the sport |
| Musculoskeletal care | A physiotherapist they self-refer to, episodically |
| Nutrition | Nobody, or the internet |
| Mental skills | Nobody |
| Load, total | Nobody. The athlete is the only one who can see it |
| The calendar | The athlete, and their league |

Two things follow. The roles do not disappear, they collapse into the athlete,
who is the least neutral person available and the least trained for it. And the
boundaries do not collapse with them: an athlete without a dietitian still has a
nutrition boundary, with nobody on the other side. Slice 12's do, don't and refer
table is the single-operator version of section 3.

## 6. Scale-only roles versus distinct-knowledge roles

The test: would this role exist for one athlete with unlimited access to
expertise and no organisation around them? Output that is a judgment nobody else
present is trained to make is knowledge. Output that is coordination, a reporting
line or liability containment is scale.

**Distinct knowledge.** Sport coach, physician, physiotherapist or athletic
trainer, strength and conditioning coach, dietitian, licensed mental health
clinician, sport scientist. Each is a body of knowledge with its own credential,
its own training route, and its own decision that nobody else may make. The
evidence that these are real rather than conventional is that the credentials are
defined externally: a medical licence, state licensing boards for psychological
services (`ccbf7536`), and the CSSD credential for sports dietetics
(`7e25530499`). None of those bodies takes instruction from a sports
organisation.

**Scale.** These describe what an institution needs, not what a person knows, and
the proof is provenance. Their boundary with every knowledge role is the same:
they own the structure, never the call.

| Role | What it actually is |
|---|---|
| Athletics health care administrator | An NCAA creation. Oversees the delivery of care so that the clinicians' authority survives contact with the athletics department (`2f487dd7`) |
| Medical director | A designation, not a discipline. Institutions "designate a licensed physician (M.D. or D.O.)" to oversee the medical tasks of all primary athletics health care providers; the same person may also be a team physician (`2f487dd7`) |
| Athlete support personnel | A rule-book category. The Code sweeps up any coach, trainer, manager, agent, team staff, official, medical or paramedical person, parent, or anyone else assisting an athlete, and binds them all ([USADA](https://www.usada.org/spirit-of-sport/athlete-support-responsibilities/), stored `7b258448`) |
| Team manager, welfare officer, agent | Logistics, safeguarding, contracts. Real work, no distinct performance knowledge |


**Argued, not obvious.** Three will not sit still. The *performance director*
looks like pure scale, but the model gives the role a decision nobody else can
make, choosing a documented path when medicine and coaching disagree
(`95287e1f`): scale can manufacture authority without manufacturing expertise.
The *mental performance consultant* sits on the line by construction, because the
handbook's own list of contributing disciplines runs from clinical psychology to
industrial-organisational psychology to sport science (`ccbf7536`), a
certification assembled around a job rather than one body of knowledge, and AASP
certification is a prerequisite for the US Olympic registry (`ccbf7536`), so an
institution made the credential load-bearing. The *performance analyst* holds
real knowledge but only separates from the coaching staff above a data volume one
person cannot process.

**Why a skill author should care.** The scale roles are the ones an agent must
never simulate, because their function is to be a specific accountable human
inside a specific organisation. The knowledge roles are where the boundary
matters, because an agent can plausibly perform one or two and must refuse the
rest.

## 7. Open questions

1. No stored source draws the rehabilitation-to-reconditioning handover between
   physiotherapist and strength coach. Friction in practice, silence in the
   consensus documents read here.
2. Pro club rosters are read off the institute and college documents, not off a
   club or league staffing standard. A league medical regulation would close it.
3. The naming difference between athletic trainer, physiotherapist, athletic
   therapist and sports therapist rests on two primary documents using two
   vocabularies for one slot. The regulated-title position per country is
   UNVERIFIED and needs each regulator's own page.
4. Three documents could not be obtained: Reid, Stewart and Thorne 2004, Burns
   and Collins 2023 (both paywalled), and a 2024 narrative review of performance
   support team effectiveness (403). Attributed by title, not quoted.
5. Only the abstract of Reardon et al. 2019 is stored. The body carries referral
   pathways and confidentiality guidance this note does not cover.
6. Nobody owning total load is read off the absence of an owner, not off a source
   stating it. Marked DERIVED.
